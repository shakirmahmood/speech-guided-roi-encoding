# What the speech tells us about objects on screen

When people watch a video with someone talking, they don't look everywhere equally. They look where the
speech sends them: at the object being named, pointed at, praised or warned about. This guide explains, in
plain words, all the ways the language of a video's speech can tell us which objects on screen matter, when
they matter, and how much.

The guide is organised around eight questions. Each part answers one of them:

1. **Which object is the speech about?** How words point at an object, from naming it to never saying it.
2. **Is the object on screen now?** How words show that a mentioned object is, or isn't, in the picture.
3. **How important is the object?** How words show how strongly the speaker wants us to look.
4. **Where on screen is the object?** How words help find it in the frame.
5. **When does it matter, and for how long?** How words show when attention should start and stop.
6. **What is happening?** How actions bring objects into play and show where to look.
7. **What is meant but not said?** How knowledge, hints and context fill the gaps.
8. **How is it said?** How stress, pauses and tone of voice add to the words.

Each part lists the clues one by one. For each there are examples, an explanation of what it tells us and
why, and, in brackets after the heading, the name researchers use for it, for anyone who wants to read more.
Each part ends with "putting it together", on how its clues work together.

One rule runs through the whole guide: the words give strong hints, but the picture has the last word. Speech
can name something that isn't visible, or point at something without naming it. The clues tell us what to
expect and where to look; the frames confirm it.

## Part 1: Which object is the speech about?

People rarely name things plainly. They say "it", "this one", "the one on the left", or nothing at all. Before
anything else, we have to link the words to the object on screen they refer to (the referent). Below are the
different ways speech can point at an object, going from easy to hard.

### 1. The object is named (noun phrase, proper name)

*"The dog", "a red car", "Sarah", "the Eiffel Tower".*

The speech names the object directly, so we can search the frame for it. This is the easiest case. A proper
name like "Sarah" is harder than it looks, though: the word tells us who, but not what she looks like. To find
her, the system needs some link between the name and an appearance, for example that she is the person who
introduced herself earlier, or the only woman on screen.

### 2. The object is named only vaguely (general noun)

*"The animal", "that thing", "the stuff".*

The word gives a category or a placeholder, not a specific object. "The animal" could be any animal in the
frame. The system has to match the general word to one object on screen, using what else is said and what is
visible.

### 3. The object is pointed at instead of named (anaphora, cataphora, deixis)

*"It jumped", "she's laughing", "this one", "over here", "here it comes, the train!"*

Words like "it", "she" and "this" mean nothing on their own. To find out what they stand for, we look:

- **back in the speech**, for the last object that fits ("grab the zucchini, now slice it": "it" is the
  zucchini);
- **ahead in the speech**, when the name comes later ("here it comes, the train!");
- **at the picture**, for what the speaker is holding, pointing at or looking at ("this one").

"This" and "that" are often never followed by a name at all, so the picture is the only way to resolve them.

### 4. The object is picked out among several similar ones (descriptions, quantity)

*"The one in red", "the tall guy", "the cup next to the laptop", "the second jar", "all the birds", "both of
them".*

Here the frame holds several objects of the same kind, and the words pick out one by its colour, size,
position or order. Sometimes the words mean a group or a number instead of one object: "all the birds",
"both of them". When another object is used to find this one ("next to the laptop"), see Part 4, point 2.

### 5. Only part of an object is meant (part-whole)

*"Its tail", "the wheel", "her hands".*

The speech refers to a piece of something larger. The important region is that part, not the whole object.
So the system must first find the whole (the dog, the car, the person) and then the part within it.

### 6. The object is never named, only implied (implicit participants, metonymy)

*"She's slicing" → a knife and something being cut. "The kitchen smells amazing" → the food.*

Sometimes the object is not said at all. An action brings its tools and participants with it (Part 6 covers
this in detail), and situations bring their typical objects (Part 7, point 1). And sometimes the word used is
not the thing meant: "the kitchen smells amazing" is about the food, not the room.

### 7. The speaker or the person spoken to (first- and second-person reference, vocatives)

*"I", "look at my shoes", "John, look at this", "you".*

These words point at people in the conversation. "I" and "my shoes" mean the speaker and their belongings, so
we need to know who is talking and where they are on screen. "John, look at this" means the person being
addressed. "You" is tricky: in a video it often means the viewer, who isn't on screen at all, so there is
nothing to find.

### 8. Text or a graphic on screen

*"As the chart shows", "read the sign", "the price is on the label".*

The referent is writing or a drawing: a chart, a caption, a sign, a label. It is a region of the picture
rather than a physical object, and it needs to be sharp enough to read.

### 9. Something matters, but nothing is named (exclamations, ellipsis)

*"Look!", "Wow, gorgeous!", "Wait for it…", "Oh no!"*

The speech tells us **when** something important is happening, but not **what** it is. There is no word to link
to any object. The picture has to answer the "what": whatever is moving, being shown or held, or suddenly
appearing at that moment. Sounds without words, such as gasps and laughter, work the same way (Part 8,
point 2).

### Putting it together

Finding the referent is the first step, and every later question depends on it. Some cases need only the
words: linking "it" back to the zucchini, or reading a name. Others need the picture as well: "this one",
"the one in red", "look!". In practice the system reads the words to know what kind of object to look for,
and then uses the frames to pick the actual one.

## Part 2: Is the object on screen now?

Hearing an object's name doesn't mean the object is in the picture. People talk about things from the past,
things they imagine, things in another room, or kinds of things in general. If the system took every mentioned
object and searched the frame for it, it would sharpen the wrong region or chase something that isn't there.
So for every mention we have to ask: **is this object actually on screen right now?**

The words usually give the answer. Below are the signs that an object is *not* here, then the signs that it
*is*.

### Signs that the object is not on screen

#### 1. It belongs to another time (tense, time adverbs)

*"The dog caught one yesterday", "last week", "tomorrow we'll bake a cake".*

Past and future tense, and words like "yesterday", "later" or "next week", place the event at another time,
so the object is often not in the current picture. Two cautions:

- The tense describes the *event*, not the object. "We'll slice them with the mandolin" is about the future,
  but the mandolin can be right there in view. So tense is a hint, and the picture has the final say. (Part 5,
  point 7 covers how the form of the verb affects *when* an object matters.)
- Videos can show the past. If the footage is a flashback, "the dog caught one yesterday" may be exactly
  what's on screen.

#### 2. It means a kind of thing, not this one (generic reference)

*"Dogs are loyal", "a ratatouille has four vegetables".*

A general statement talks about a whole category, not a particular object in front of us. "Dogs are loyal"
says nothing about the dog in the frame, even if there is one.

#### 3. It is imagined (hypothetical, conditional)

*"If you had a dog…", "imagine a huge wave", "you could use a whisk", "I'd rather buy extra vegetables than
cut my finger".*

Words like "if", "imagine", "could", "would rather" set up a situation that isn't real. The objects in it are
not present, or at least not in the way described. This is easy to miss when the same kind of object is
visible: a cook's fingers may be on screen, but "cut my finger" is about a finger that might get cut, not
something to look at.

#### 4. It is said to be missing (negation)

*"There's no squirrel", "without the lid", "if you slice without a guard".*

Negation names an object precisely to say it isn't there. Searching the frame for it would be wrong.

#### 5. It is somewhere else (displaced location)

*"My dog back home", "in the other room", "at the shop".*

The object exists, but the speech places it away from the scene we see.

#### 6. It isn't meant literally (idiom, metaphor)

*"It's raining cats and dogs", "a piece of cake", "he's a couch potato".*

Idioms and metaphors use object words without meaning any object. There is no cat, cake or potato to find.

#### 7. It comes from someone else's words (reported speech)

*"She told me the dog was sick", "he said the box was heavy".*

Reported speech repeats what someone said at another time and place, so the object usually isn't the one on
screen now.

#### 8. It can't be seen at all (abstract nouns)

*"The idea", "freedom", "the economy", "the recipe".*

Abstract nouns name things with no appearance. There is nothing in the picture to point at.

### Signs that the object is on screen

#### 9. The speaker presents it (presentatives, "this" versus "that")

*"Here's the eggplant", "this is my kitchen", "as you can see", "meet Max".*

Some phrases exist precisely to show something: "here's", "this is", "as you can see", "meet". They are strong
signs that the object is in view right now, usually at that very moment. A related, weaker sign is "this"
versus "that": "this one" tends to mean something close and visible, "that one" something farther away, but
people mix them up, so it is only a soft hint.

### Putting it together

These signs are hints from the words alone. The picture still has the last word: if the object is clearly
visible, it is on screen whatever the tense; if it is nowhere in the frame, it isn't, whatever the speaker says.
In practice the system should read the words first, to know what to expect, and then check the frames.

## Part 3: How important is the object?

Once we know which object the speech is about and that it is on screen, the next question is how much it
matters. A sentence often mentions several objects, and they don't all deserve the same attention. In "the
chef slices the tomato on the board", the board is mentioned but nobody needs to look at it. The words carry
many signs of how strongly the speaker wants us to look at each object.

### 1. The speaker tells us to look (perception commands, importance markers)

*"Look at this", "watch", "check out", "see that?", "notice that", "the key part is", "pay attention to",
"here's the trick", "careful with…".*

The clearest sign of all. Words of seeing used as commands ("look", "watch", "check") send the viewer's eyes
straight to the object. Phrases like "the key part is" or "notice that" do the same without a seeing verb: the
speaker says outright that this matters. Warnings ("careful with your fingers") also send the eyes to the
danger.

### 2. It is what the sentence is about (grammatical subject)

*"**The dog** chased the squirrel into the bushes."*

The subject of a sentence, the one doing the action or being described, is usually the most important object
in it. The object of the verb (the squirrel) comes next, and places and background (the bushes) come last.
Inside a longer description, the main word matters more than the words describing it: in "the bowl of
tomatoes", the bowl is what is meant, and the tomatoes only help identify it.

**A caution: when the subject is the speaker.** Narrators keep saying "I", "we" and "you" while describing what
they do to something else. In "**I'll** move on now to the yellow squash", "I" is the subject, but nobody needs
to look at the speaker to follow the sentence; the eye goes to the squash. In "**I** tend to always end early"
(said while slicing), the sentence is about where the slicing stops, the leftover end of the vegetable in the
cook's hand. So when the subject is the speaker, "we", or "you" meaning the viewer, the object of the verb
usually matters more. The speaker counts as the main object only when the sentence is about them: how they
look, what they wear, what happens to them ("I just cut my hair", "I'm soaked!").

### 3. The sentence puts it in the spotlight (focus constructions)

*"It's **the dog** that caught it", "That dog, I love", "What's he holding? **A snake!**"*

Speakers can rearrange a sentence to put one object in the spotlight: "it's the dog that…" or moving it to the
front. A question followed by its answer does the same: the answer is the point of the exchange.

### 4. It is set against something else (contrast)

*"Not the cat, **the dog**", "the left one, not the right one".*

When the speaker contrasts two objects, the one they choose matters and the one they reject doesn't. The
rejected object is mentioned only to be dismissed. In speech, contrast usually comes with stress on the chosen
word (Part 8, point 1).

### 5. It is new (new versus given information)

*First mention: "Here's **a mandolin**." Later: "it", "the device".*

An object introduced for the first time draws more attention than one already being talked about, because the
viewer has to find it. Later mentions keep it active but need less effort, since the viewer already knows
where it is.

### 6. It is mentioned again and again (repetition)

*A product named in nearly every sentence of a review.*

Counting how often an object is mentioned shows which ones the video is really about. An object named many
times is a main subject; one named once is a passing detail. (How a topic stays important between mentions is
in Part 7, point 6.)

### 7. The speaker has feelings about it (evaluative language, intensifiers)

*"Beautiful", "gross", "perfect", "fun to watch", "**so** cool", "really", "absolutely stunning".*

When the speaker judges an object (beautiful, gross, perfect) or uses strong words about it (so, really,
absolutely), that object matters to them, and viewers follow. Exclamations that name nothing, like "whoa!",
are covered in Part 1, point 9.

### 8. It is singled out by comparison (superlatives, comparatives)

*"The **biggest** one", "faster than the red car", "the **only** one with a lid".*

Superlatives and comparisons pick one object out of several and make it stand out. The winner of the
comparison ("the biggest one", the faster car) matters most; the others are there to compare against.

### 9. It is one of a list (enumeration)

*"You'll need flour, eggs and butter."*

In a list, each item gets attention in turn, usually as it is named or shown. No single item is the most
important; importance moves along the list.

### Putting it together

These signs add up. "Look at **this** beautiful tomato" has a command to look, a pointing word and a judgement,
so the tomato matters a lot. "The tomato is on the board" has none of them beyond the tomato being the subject.
Importance is also relative within a sentence: the subject usually comes first, then the object of the verb,
then the rest, except when the subject is just the speaker narrating.

## Part 4: Where on screen is the object?

Knowing which object is meant is not always enough to find it. The frame may hold several similar objects, the
object may be small, or it may be moving. Speakers often add words that say where to look, and these narrow
the search to one part of the picture.

### 1. A place in the frame (screen-based location)

*"On the left", "top right", "in the background", "in the corner".*

The speaker names a part of the picture itself. This is the most direct help: the system can search that area
first, or choose the matching object when there are several.

### 2. Next to another object (landmark)

*"Under the table", "next to the laptop", "behind the door", "on the shelf".*

The object is placed relative to another one, the landmark. The system first finds the landmark (the table,
the laptop), then looks around it. The landmark is only a guide: it helps the search but matters less than the
object being located.

### 3. Relative to the speaker (speaker-based location)

*"Behind me", "in my hand", "to my right", "this one here".*

The speaker uses their own body as the reference point, so we need to know where the speaker is on screen. One
trap: when a person faces the camera, their right is the viewer's left. "To my right" means the left side of
the picture.

### 4. How big or how far away (size and distance)

*"Tiny", "huge", "way off in the distance", "up close", "an eighth of an inch thick".*

Words about size and distance tell us how large the object will look in the frame. "Way off in the distance"
means a small object, often near the horizon; "up close" means a large one filling much of the picture. This
helps pick the right object and decide how big the region to sharpen should be.

### 5. Where it is going (direction of motion)

*"Coming toward us", "flying off to the left", "rolling under the couch".*

For a moving object, the speech can say which way it is heading. That tells us where it will be in the next
moments, so the sharp region can follow it, or even be placed slightly ahead of it.

### Putting it together

These words don't make an object more or less important; they help find it. They matter most when the picture
is busy, when there are several objects of the same kind, or when the object is small. They also depend on the
picture: "on the left" only makes sense once we know what the frame shows, and "behind me" once we know where
the speaker stands.

## Part 5: When does the object matter, and for how long?

Importance changes from moment to moment. An object can matter for a second and then not at all, or stay
important for a whole scene. Knowing which object matters is only half the job; we also need to know when to
start sharpening it and when to stop. The speech gives many signs about timing.

### 1. The moment it is named (word timing)

*The instant the word "carrot" is spoken.*

The simplest anchor is the time the object's name is said. Viewers react fast: in eye-tracking experiments,
people start looking at a named object about a fifth of a second after the word begins. So importance should
start at, or just after, the word.

### 2. Words that mean "right now"

*"Right now", "at this moment", "now", "here it is".*

These say the object matters at the very moment of speaking, not earlier or later.

### 3. Something is about to happen (anticipation)

*"Watch what happens next", "wait for it", "in a second", "is about to".*

The speaker warns that an event is coming. Importance should rise before the event and hold until it happens,
so the viewer is already looking when it does. Verbs can also make viewers look ahead before anything is named
(Part 6, point 2).

### 4. Something just happened (looking back)

*"Did you see that?!", "that was close", "wow, look what it did".*

The speaker reacts after the event. The words come too late for live viewing, but because the video is
processed before it is streamed, we can go back and make the object sharp during the moment that just passed,
before the words were spoken.

### 5. The order of events (sequence words)

*"First", "then", "after that", "finally", "next".*

Sequence words say one thing follows another. Importance moves from one object to the next in that order:
"first the onion, then the garlic" means the onion now and the garlic after.

### 6. How long it lasts (duration)

*"Keep watching", "the whole time", "take this nice and slow" versus "blink and you'll miss it".*

Some words say the object matters for a long stretch, others that it matters only for an instant. This
decides whether the sharp region should stay on for many seconds or just a moment.

### 7. Whether the action is happening, done or coming (aspect)

*"Is catching" versus "caught" versus "is about to catch".*

The form of the verb says where the action is in time: happening now, already finished, or still ahead. An
action happening now makes its objects important now; a finished one may not; an upcoming one makes them
important soon.

### When importance ends

The speech rarely says when to stop. Reasonable signs are: the next object is named, the sentence or topic
ends, the camera cuts to another shot, or the object leaves the frame. Without some rule like this, every
object ever named would stay sharp forever.

## Part 6: What is happening?

Speech often describes an action rather than an object: someone slices, pours, throws or catches. Actions
matter for two reasons. They tell us which objects take part, even ones that are never named. And they tell us
where the interesting part of the picture is, because viewers look at where the action happens, not just at the
objects one by one.

### 1. An action and who takes part (semantic roles)

*"The dog is **catching** the ball", "she's **pouring** the milk", "she's **slicing**".*

An action verb brings its participants with it: who does it (the dog), what it is done to (the ball), what it is
done with (a knife, for slicing), and often the place where they meet (the dog's mouth and the ball in the air).
The participants count even when they are not named: "she's slicing" brings a knife and something being cut.
The meeting point is usually where viewers look. Action verbs also mean movement, and moving things draw the eye.

### 2. The action predicts what comes next (anticipatory looks)

*"Now the boy will **eat**…", "let's **slice**…".*

Listeners don't wait for the object to be named. On hearing "eat", they already look at the food; on hearing
"slice", at the knife and the vegetable. So an action can make an object important before it is mentioned at all.

### 3. Something changes (change of state)

*"It's **melting**", "watch it **explode**", "it's **turning brown**".*

When the speech says something is changing, the region where the change happens is the one to watch: the
melting edge, the browning surface, the spot about to burst.

### 4. Something moves from one place to another (path)

*"He **throws** the ball **to her**", "pass me the salt", "put it in the oven".*

These actions have a starting point, a path and an end point: the thrower, the ball in flight, the person
catching it. Importance moves along that path with the object.

### 5. Hands working on an object (manipulation)

*"**Grab** the screw", "**cut** along the line", "**press** firmly", "**hold** it like this".*

In how-to videos, the speaker describes what their hands are doing. The hands and the tool or object they are
handling are the centre of attention, often closer than anything else in the frame.

### 6. The result to look at (result state)

*"See how **smooth** it is now", "look how **even** these are", "it's **done**".*

After an action, the speaker may point to its result. The finished object, or the part that changed, is the
thing to inspect, and the viewer looks at it closely.

### Putting it together

Actions are where most objects that are never named come from: a slice needs a knife and something to cut, a
throw needs a ball. They also say where the action is in the frame, which may be a small region between objects
rather than any single object. That region is often the best place to spend extra quality.

## Part 7: What is meant but not said?

Much of what people mean is never put into words. Listeners fill the gaps with what they know about the world,
about the situation, and about why the speaker is talking at all. A system that reads only the literal words
misses this, so it needs the same kind of background knowledge a viewer brings.

### 1. Knowing how the world works (world knowledge, scripts)

*"The kettle's whistling" → the kettle. "Goal!" → the ball, the net, the players. "Dinner's ready" → the food
on the table.*

Everyday situations come with familiar objects. A whistling sound means a kettle; a goal means a ball going into
a net. Listeners picture these objects without being told, and look for them. The system needs this same
knowledge to know what to search for.

### 2. A hint instead of a statement (implicature)

*"Careful!", "well, that didn't go as planned", "it's getting a bit hot in here".*

People often hint rather than say things outright. "Careful!" means there is a danger in view: a blade, a hot
pan, an edge. "That didn't go as planned" means something visibly failed: a spill, a collapse, a burnt dish. The
words name nothing, but they tell the viewer what kind of thing to look for.

### 3. Saying the opposite (irony, sarcasm)

*"Oh great, just perfect", "well, that's lovely" (when it isn't).*

In sarcasm the words mean the reverse of what they say. "Just perfect" after a mishap means something went
wrong, and the thing that went wrong is what to look at. Tone of voice usually gives it away (Part 8), which a
written transcript doesn't carry.

### 4. Why the speaker is talking (speaker intent)

*An advert, a lesson, a story, a review.*

The purpose of the talk shapes what matters. In an advert, the product. In a lesson, the thing being explained
or demonstrated. In a story, the characters and what happens to them. In a review, the item being judged and its
features.

### 5. Who talks next, and who reacts (turn-taking)

*Two people in conversation, taking turns.*

In dialogue, attention moves between speakers: viewers look at whoever is talking, and often at the listener's
face when something surprising is said. The turn-taking itself says where to look next.

### 6. The bigger picture (discourse topic, genre)

*A segment about baking a cake; a cooking show; a sports broadcast.*

The topic of the moment and the kind of video set expectations that last beyond any single sentence. While the
topic is the cake, the cake stays important even in sentences that don't mention it. And the kind of video tells
us what usually matters: the ball and players in sports, the food and hands in cooking, the speaker's face in a
vlog.

### Putting it together

These signs are the hardest to read, because nothing in the words points directly at an object. They depend on
knowledge and on understanding the situation, which is why a language model is useful here: it has read enough
to know that "Careful!" next to a blade is about the blade.

## Part 8: How is it said?

The same words can mean different things depending on how they are spoken. Stress, loudness, pitch, pauses and
the feeling in a voice all tell listeners what matters. None of this survives in a written transcript, so a
system that only reads the text misses it. These clues need the sound itself.

### 1. Stress on a word (prosodic stress)

*"Take the **red** one", "it's the **dog**, not the cat".*

Speakers stress the word that matters most, by saying it louder, longer or with a change in pitch. Stress on
"red" tells the listener that colour is the point: among several, take the red one. Experiments show that
stress makes listeners find the right object faster, especially when it is being contrasted with another.

### 2. Sounds without words (nonverbal vocalisations)

*Laughter, gasps, screams, "ooh", "aah".*

These sounds are reactions to something, usually something seen. Like "Look!" or "Wow!" (Part 1, point 9),
they say that something notable is happening right now but not what it is, so the picture has to show the cause.

### 3. Whispering or shouting (vocal effort)

*A whisper before something delicate; a shout when something goes wrong.*

How loud and tense the voice is shows how intense the moment is. A whisper builds suspense and draws viewers in;
a shout signals urgency. Both raise attention, in different ways.

### 4. Pauses

*A dramatic pause before a reveal: "And the winner is… [pause]".*

A pause right before something is shown or named tells viewers that an important moment is coming. It works like
"wait for it" without the words.

### 5. Speed of speech (speech rate)

*Fast, excited speech versus slow, careful speech.*

Speaking fast often shows excitement; slowing down shows emphasis, as if to say "pay attention to this part".
Both mark a stretch of the video as more important than the rest.

### 6. Feeling in the voice (vocal emotion)

*Excited, scared, disgusted, amazed.*

The emotion in a voice shows how strongly the speaker reacts, and viewers tend to share it. A disgusted or amazed
voice suggests there is something on screen worth reacting to.

### 7. Hesitation before a name (disfluency)

*"Pass me the, uh, the thing", "it's a, um, a spatula".*

When a speaker hesitates with "uh" or "um", listeners expect something new or hard to name, and start looking
for an object they haven't seen discussed yet.

### Putting it together

These clues come only from the sound. A system that reads a transcript can't use any of them. To use them, it
would need to measure them from the audio (which words are stressed, where the pauses are, how excited the voice
sounds) and pass that along with the words. Many transcription tools also drop "uh" and "um", so even hesitation
is lost unless the transcriber keeps it.
